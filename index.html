Continue A, or listen to B? A listening pilot for PMus

Overview

The question. PMus-Self and PMus-Partner are meant to capture two different relations between a continuation of player A and the duo's shared past: how much the continuation carries on A's own playing (Self: what A's past adds beyond B's), and how much it responds to B (Partner: what B's past adds beyond A's). If the two relations can be heard separately, there should be continuations that listeners judge as continuing A but not as responding to B, and the reverse; and Self and Partner should split on those continuations the same way.

Why the paper's listening study could not test this. In that study, every judged pair consisted of two continuations generated with access to both players. On those pairs PMus-Self and PMus-Partner rarely disagreed by more than noise, and listeners answered the two questions alike. Whether listeners separate the two relations, and whether Self and Partner follow that separation, therefore remained open.

This pilot. We composed duo excerpts in which the two relations are pulled apart. Each stimulus has a 10 s shared past of player A and player B, followed by two alternative 5 s continuations of player A only:

  • XA, “continues A”: develops A's own phrase or behaviour and does not take up a cue that B introduced near the end of the past.
  • XB, “listens and responds to B”: takes up B's cue (a key change, register, rhythm, cadence, motif, texture, a new figure, or a groove) and drops or redirects A's phrase.

In three examples (2, 3 and 9) the change near the end of the past comes from A instead; there XA carries A's new idea forward and XB goes along with what B keeps playing. Both continuations are meant to be acceptable music: the contrast is in the relation, not in quality. Items were composed in MIDI, rendered with a General MIDI soundfont, and curated by listening.

PMus on these items.

Design. Musically trained listeners (at least 3 years of training) heard a practice example and then 10 examples. Each example came with one of two questions, five examples per question, interleaved in random order, with a question card before every example. No listener heard the same example under both questions. They were told explicitly not to answer which track sounds better overall. The construction predicts XA under the continue-A question and XB under the listen-to-B question; per listener, the gap P(XA | continue-A) − P(XA | listen-to-B) should be positive.

The listening task, as participants saw it

The instructions below are reproduced verbatim from the participant page, without the consent, screening and headphone-check parts (participants reported their years of musical training, were screened at 3 years, and passed a headphone check before the practice example).

Participant instructions

Welcome!

In this experiment you will hear short excerpts of two musicians playing together, Player A and Player B, each followed by two possible ways the music could continue. Your task will be to answer a question about these two continuations by choosing one of them. Please listen closely and attentively.

Each excerpt, in 3 steps
  1. Listen to the duo: Player A and Player B alone (a few seconds each), then both together.
  2. Listen to the two continuations: each alone or right after the duo (the yellow part).
  3. Answer the question: choose Track 1 or Track 2. There is no right or wrong answer.
The two questions

Each excerpt comes with one of these two questions, in no fixed order: please read it every time.

Question about player AWhich track continues player A better?= which track best carries on Player A's own line
Question about player BWhich track listens and responds to player B better?= which track best reacts to what Player B was playing
Important: we are not asking which track sounds better overall. Each question asks about one specific relation: answer only that question, even if you prefer the other track.

On every example

  • A question card came first, showing the example's question and one reminder line: for continue-A, “Not which track is better overall, but which one best carries on Player A's own line.”; for listen-to-B, “Not which track is better overall, but which one best listens and responds to what Player B was playing.” When the question differed from the previous example, the card said so, showed the previous question crossed out, and its button became active after 3 s.
  • The page showed the question at the top in its colour (blue for A, magenta for B), outlined the player the question is about, and repeated the question on the answer buttons.
  • Gating: the tracks unlocked once “Both together” had played to the end and Player A and Player B had each played for at least 2 s; the answer unlocked once each track had been played to the end, alone or after the duo. Which continuation was Track 1 varied across listeners (roughly balanced per example and question).
  • In “Duo, then the track” the continuation starts at 10 s; the bar and the card turn yellow when it begins. This was the version participants used most.
  • In the later sessions (L7, L8 and the excluded listener), two optional free-text questions after the 10 examples asked how each question had been understood.

Try it

Every example below uses the exact audio files participants heard (only file names and metadata changed). Track order is randomised each time the page loads. Choose a question, listen, answer, then reveal which track was which, the construction, the PMus scores and what listeners chose. Nothing is locked here; participants had to follow the gating described above. Keys: 1/2 play the duo then Track 1/2, space stops.

Results

Sessions counted: complete sessions with the interleaved two-question design, not duplicates. Listeners are numbered L1–L8 in the order they started. Four are colleagues (musicians known to the authors, recruited directly) and four were recruited on an online panel.

One listener excluded. One colleague is excluded from the main analysis: in the free-text answer at the end, they described judging which continuation was the most idiomatically appropriate, rather than the relation asked about. This exclusion was decided after reading the free-text answers, so totals with this listener included are shown as well.

Overall

“Matched” counts answers that agree with the construction (XA under continue-A, XB under listen-to-B). Chance is half.

Per listener

Gap = P(XA | continue-A question) − P(XA | listen-to-B question), from 5 answers per question. +1 = fully as constructed, 0 = the question made no difference, −1 = fully reversed. One online-panel listener answered almost exactly in reverse (1/10 matched).

Per example

ΔSelf = Self(XA) − Self(XB); ΔPartner = Partner(XB) − Partner(XA); both at the 5 s past (the paper's protocol), in nats/token: positive = the measure prefers the constructed track. Listener columns exclude the excluded listener. Example 2 was heard only by L1 and replaced by Example 11 for everyone else.

Statistics

PMus beyond the pilot items

On all 46 constructed pairs built without using PMus (the composed originals, of which the pilot uses two), PMus-Self and PMus-Partner both ranked the pair as constructed (Self prefers XA and Partner prefers XB) in 40/46 at the 5 s past (33/46 at the 10 s past).

How PMus scores a continuation

Each continuation X is scored by its mean negative log-likelihood h(X | context) under an external audio language model (MusicGen-large), in nats per token, with C the true shared past (A and B mixed). Every measure is a difference h(X | comparison) − h(X | C); a higher value means the true context makes X more predictable than the comparison does.

  • Self = h(X | B) − h(X | C): what A's past adds beyond B's.
  • Partner = h(X | A) − h(X | C): what B's past adds beyond A's.
  • Partner+ = h(X | A mixed with B̄) − h(X | C): the true B against the B parts of other excerpts (B̄), A kept; mean over 8 decoys.
  • Duo = h(X | C̄) − h(X | C): the true shared past against the full pasts of other excerpts (C̄); mean over 8 decoys. A shared-context measure: it is not designed to separate the two relations.

Scores are given with the 5 s past (the last 5 s of the shared past as context, the paper's protocol) and the full 10 s past that listeners heard.

Limits

  • Small pilot. 8 listeners (9 with the excluded one), 5 answers per question each. Data collection is continuing.
  • The planned evidence threshold has not been reached. The planned test (a one-sided Bayes factor on per-listener gaps, threshold 6) gives 2.0 for all 8 listeners, and the first planned look is at 16 listeners. The group values are descriptive.
  • Items were selected for large PMus splits. Of the ten examples heard by all listeners after the first, two are composed originals (Examples 3 and 6), two are PMus-tuned copies of originals (4 and 9) and six are variations checked with PMus while composing (1, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11). These items are a best case for the measures, not a typical case. PMus was used to build and select items, not to score listeners.
  • Colleagues are not naive listeners. They know the authors and may know the aim of the study.
  • The analyses marked “added after seeing the data” (the two-part mixture model, the colleague/panel split, the exclusion) are exploratory.
  • Stimuli are rendered MIDI with one General MIDI soundfont, not recordings.
